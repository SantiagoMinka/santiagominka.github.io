§1

Commit protocols

Core idea. Atomic commit: every node votes; commit only if all vote commit; any crash leads to abort. It differs from consensus, where nodes agree on one of the proposed values and a majority is enough. 2PC is safe but blocking. 3PC adds a phase to avoid blocking, at a higher cost.

Q1Describe

Describe two-phase commit (coordinator and participant states). Analyze what happens when a participant or the coordinator fails. Why is 2PC called a blocking protocol?

Model answer

Phase 1 (voting). The coordinator writes start-2PC to its log and sends prepare; each participant votes vote-commit or vote-abort (and logs it). Phase 2 (decision). If all voted commit, the coordinator logs and sends global-commit, else global-abort. Participants log the decision and ack.

States: coordinator Init → Wait → Commit/Abort; participant Init → Ready → Commit/Abort.

Participant fails: after a timeout in Wait, the coordinator decides abort.

Coordinator fails:

  • Participant in Init (no prepare yet): it can abort; nobody can have received commit.
  • Participant in Ready: it cannot decide alone. It asks the other participants: if one got the decision, follow it; if one is in Init, the coordinator crashed before the end of the prepare phase → abort. If all are in Ready, nothing can be decided until the coordinator recovers: the protocol blocks.

Why

A participant in Ready promised to commit if asked, so it cannot abort; but it does not know the decision, so it cannot commit. 2PC is always safe, but it gives up liveness, and resources stay locked during the block.

Q2Compare

How does three-phase commit avoid blocking? List the recovery rules of a participant when the coordinator fails, and compare 3PC with 2PC.

Model answer

3PC splits the decision in two: first tell everyone the outcome (prepare-commit → state Pre-commit, ack ready-commit), then commit. Conditions for non-blocking: no state leads directly to both commit and abort, and no state where a decision is impossible can lead to commit.

Coordinator fails, participant in Ready asks the others:

  • someone in Abort → abort; someone in Init → abort;
  • someone in Commit → commit;
  • someone in Pre-commit, and Pre-commit + Ready form a majority → commit;
  • a majority in Ready, nobody in Pre-commit → abort.

No two participants can be one in Pre-commit and the other in Init.

Why

Pre-commit is a "decision announced" state. If any survivor saw it, everybody knows the decision was commit; if nobody saw it, nobody can have committed. FLP still applies: with partitions in an asynchronous system, you cannot have both safety and liveness.

Q3Describe

State the CAP theorem and explain its practical meaning for modern data systems.

Model answer

A system that stores replicated or partitioned data can have at most two of: Consistency (as if there were one up-to-date copy), Availability (reads and writes always answered), Partition tolerance.

Partitions can always happen, so the real choice is: during a partition, stay consistent (refuse some requests) or stay available (accept requests and risk divergence). Modern systems choose a balance for each application, often with weaker consistency models (chapter 08).

Why

During a partition, a replica that accepts a write cannot tell the other side. Either it answers (available but inconsistent) or it waits (consistent but unavailable). There is no third option.

§2

Raft and replicated state machines

Core idea. A replicated state machine: identical copies apply the same commands in the same order, so clients see one fault-tolerant machine. Raft keeps a replicated log with a leader. It is equivalent to multi-Paxos but designed to be understandable: log replication, leader election, safety.

Q4DescribeExam 18 Nov 2023 · 6 Sep 2024

Consider the Raft consensus protocol. Which problem does it solve? Under which assumptions? Does it guarantee safety (it is always correct) and liveness (it always makes progress)? Motivate your answers.

Model answer

Problem. Consensus on a sequence of commands for a replicated state machine: all non-failing machines execute the same commands in the same order.

Assumptions. Asynchronous, unreliable communication (messages can be delayed, duplicated, lost). Processes fail by crashing and may restart; they record their state on durable storage. No Byzantine failures.

How (short). Followers, candidates, leader. Time is divided into terms. A follower that hears no heartbeat within a randomized timeout (150–300 ms) becomes a candidate, increments the term, votes for itself and sends RequestVote. A majority of votes makes it leader (one vote per server per term). The leader appends client commands to its log and sends AppendEntries; an entry replicated on a majority is committed and executed.

Safety: yes, always.

  • At most one leader per term (one vote per term, majority needed).
  • Log matching: AppendEntries carries the index and term of the previous entry; a follower rejects mismatches, and the leader retries with lower indexes. So equal index and term mean identical logs up to that point.
  • Leader completeness: a candidate includes its last index and term; voters refuse candidates with less up-to-date logs. So every new leader has all committed entries.

Liveness: not guaranteed in theory (FLP: deterministic consensus cannot also guarantee termination in an asynchronous system with a crash). Split votes can in principle repeat forever. In practice, randomized timeouts make this very unlikely, and the system makes progress whenever a majority is up and can communicate, with timeouts much longer than message delays.

Why

Raft puts safety in the rules on terms, votes and logs, which never depend on timing. Timing is used only for liveness (timeouts). This is the standard way around FLP: never be wrong, and be fast most of the time.

Q5ScenarioExam 17 Jan 2024 · 12 Jul 2024

A simple data store: (a) on a single machine; (b) replicated on 5 machines with Raft. Compare the two in terms of response time for client requests, replication consistency, and fault tolerance.

Why

Raft does not add performance, it adds availability and durability. The cost is the majority round trip on every write. With 2f + 1 replicas Raft tolerates f crashes.

Q6Exercise

5 nodes A–E; A is the leader of term 4. A partition separates {A, B} from {C, D, E}. Describe what happens in both sides, and after the partition heals.

Model answer

  1. A and B still think A is leader of term 4. C, D, E stop receiving heartbeats.
  2. One of C, D, E times out first, say D: it becomes candidate for term 5 and gets votes from C and E (3 of 5): D is leader of term 5.
  3. Clients that write to A: A appends to its log but cannot reach a majority, so nothing commits and clients get no success.
  4. Clients that write to D: D replicates on C, E and commits.
  5. When the partition heals, A receives a message with term 5 > 4: it learns it is out of date and steps down to follower. Its uncommitted entries are overwritten by D's log through the consistency check.

Why

Two leaders can exist at the same time, but in different terms, and only the one with a majority can commit. Terms identify obsolete information, so the old leader is safe: it cannot do harm.

Q7Exercise

Leader log (index: term): 1:1, 2:1, 3:2, 4:3. A follower has 1:1, 2:1, 3:1, 4:1. The leader sends AppendEntries for index 4 with previous entry ⟨3, 2⟩. What happens step by step?

Model answer

  1. The follower checks its entry at index 3: term 1 ≠ 2. It rejects.
  2. The leader retries with a lower index: send entries 3–4 with previous ⟨2, 1⟩.
  3. The follower has index 2 with term 1: match. It deletes its conflicting entries 3:1 and 4:1 and appends 3:2, 4:3.
  4. Now the follower's log equals the leader's.

Why

This is an induction: if logs match at the previous entry, appending the same new entry keeps them matching. The leader always assumes its log is correct; normal operation repairs the followers, with no special recovery code. The follower's entries 3:1 and 4:1 were never committed (otherwise this leader would have them).

Q8Describe

How do modern distributed databases (e.g. Spanner) combine replicated state machines and 2PC?

Model answer

The database is partitioned; each partition is replicated with Paxos/Raft, so a partition behaves like one machine that does not fail (5 replicas survive 2 crashes). Across partitions, 2PC gives atomic commit. The 2PC coordinator and participants are themselves replicated groups, so the blocking case of 2PC (coordinator crash) practically disappears, and channels can be assumed reliable as long as a majority of each partition is reachable.

Why

2PC is blocking only because a single node can fail. Replace each node with a replicated state machine and the weak point is gone. The two layers solve two different problems: consensus inside a partition, atomic commit across partitions.

§3

Byzantine conditions: blockchains

Core idea. BFT replication needs 3f + 1 nodes for f traitors. Permissionless blockchains (Bitcoin) work with an unknown, very large set of nodes. Proof of work orders blocks; guarantees are only probabilistic.

Q9Describe

Explain how a proof-of-work blockchain acts as a replicated state machine in a Byzantine environment. What are its assumptions and guarantees? What is double spending?

Model answer

State: the balance of each user. Log: the ledger of signed transactions, grouped in blocks, replicated by all nodes.

Miners collect pending transactions in a block and search a proof of work over the chain + the new block: hard to find (brute force, about one every 10 minutes, difficulty adapted to the computing power), easy to verify. The winner broadcasts the block; others verify it and start on the next one. This gives global agreement on the order of blocks. If two chains appear, the one that grows faster (longer) survives.

Assumptions: very many nodes; unknown membership; no entity controls the majority of computing power. Guarantees: only probabilistic; a longer chain that you do not know may always exist.

Double spending: an attacker with enough computing power builds two concurrent chains, one paying A and one paying B, and shows each to its target. A and B can never be 100% sure that a conflicting chain does not exist; waiting for more blocks lowers the risk.

Why

With unknown membership you cannot count votes (anyone could create many identities). Proof of work makes a "vote" cost computing power, so the majority of power decides, not the majority of identities.
